Lateral Epicondylalgia · Elbow Program

Elbow Tendon Loading Program

An 8-week progressive loading plan for persistent tennis elbow, built to run alongside the shoulder program without the two fighting each other.

8 months persistent FlexBar · bands · dumbbells Grip-sparing shoulder swaps included

Ground Rules

  • The 24-hour rule governs everything. Pain during loading up to 4/10 is acceptable and expected. What matters is the next morning — if the elbow is clearly worse 24 hours later, that dose was too much. Repeat the same dose, don't advance.
  • Do not rest it completely. Unloading a tendinopathy makes the tendon weaker and sets up the next flare. The plan is to change what loads it, not to stop loading it.
  • Consistency beats intensity. Tendons respond to accumulated load over months. Missing sessions costs you far more than using a lighter weight.
  • Progress on time, not on feel. Symptoms improve before capacity does — that's exactly how you got to eight months. Stay on each phase for its full two weeks even if it feels fine.
  • One variable at a time. Don't add weight, add reps, and reintroduce a grip in the same week. You won't know what caused a flare.
  • Track it. Note morning stiffness on a 0–10 scale each day. Over eight weeks the trend line is the only honest measure — day-to-day noise will mislead you.

Grip-Sparing Swaps for the Shoulder Program

Gripping is a wrist extensor exercise — the extensors contract isometrically to stop the wrist collapsing into flexion when the fingers pull. That's why a grip-strength test reproduces tennis elbow pain. Your shoulder program is largely cable-and-handle work, so it has been quietly dosing that tendon several times a week without either of us counting it.

None of this means stopping the shoulder work. It means separating the grip from it for the first few weeks:

  • Lat Pull Down, Seated Row, Face Pulls — the worst offenders, because grip is sustained under real load. Use lifting straps. This is the single highest-value change on the page; straps remove nearly all extensor demand while leaving the shoulder work identical.
  • Cable External / Internal Rotation, Cable Front Raise, Active Assisted Flexion & Abduction — swap the handle for a D-ring ankle cuff strapped around your forearm just above the wrist. The exercise is unchanged; your hand simply isn't in it.
  • Band Pull-Aparts — loop the band around your wrists instead of holding it.
  • Landmine Press, Half-Kneeling Press, Incline Press, Triceps Extension, Prone Swiss Ball work — these need a hand. Keep them, but hold at the minimum grip effort the lift allows and keep the wrist stacked neutral, not extended. Most people death-grip out of habit; if you can't loosen it, the weight is too heavy for now.
  • Supinated Bicep Curls — leave these alone. Palm-up curls load the biceps and the wrist flexors, not the extensors. They're one of the few things in your program that isn't a problem.
  • Scaption at 1–3 lb — fine as is. The load is too light to matter.

On thicker grips: a fatter handle genuinely helps for twisting tasks — a screwdriver, a scraper, a racquet — because it cuts the extensor co-contraction needed to resist the tool rotating in your hand. It does not help on a straight cable pull, where it just makes the grip harder. Use fat grips on tools, straps on the pulling machines.

How the Phases Work

Weeks 1–2 · Isometric. Sustained holds reliably reduce tendon pain and keep load going through the tissue while it settles. Daily, and they double as a warm-up before anything provocative.

Weeks 3–4 · Eccentric. Slow lowering under load. This is the best-evidenced intervention for lateral epicondylalgia — the FlexBar "Tyler Twist" protocol is the specific version that has been studied.

Weeks 5–6 · Heavy Slow Resistance. Full lift-and-lower with real weight and a 3-seconds-up, 3-seconds-down tempo. Eccentrics reduce pain; heavy slow work is what actually rebuilds capacity and stops the next flare.

Weeks 7–8 · Load & task return. Grip comes back, the shoulder program returns to normal handles, and you deliberately re-expose the elbow to the real-world tasks that provoked it — in graded doses rather than an afternoon of rust scraping.

The elbow work is short: 10–15 minutes, done separately from or after your shoulder session, never before it.

Week 1 — Isometric Phase
Settle the irritation without unloading the tendon. Isometrics are the workhorse here: they reduce tendon pain reliably and keep load going through the tissue. Daily, every day.
01

Isometric Wrist Extension Hold

5 × 45 sec · dailyNew
Elbow bent 90° · light dumbbell or self-resistance

Setup

Sit with your forearm resting flat on a table, palm facing down, hand hanging just off the edge. Elbow bent to about 90°. Hold a light dumbbell (1–3 lb), or press the back of your hand up into your other hand for self-resistance.

Movement

Lift the wrist into about 15–20° of extension and then hold it dead still for 45 seconds. There is no movement in this exercise — the whole point is a sustained, unchanging contraction. Rest 60 seconds between holds.

Form Check

The forearm stays glued to the table — if it lifts, you're using your shoulder. Keep the fingers relaxed and slightly open; curling them into a fist turns this into a grip exercise as well.

Tip Aim for a load that produces about 3–4/10 discomfort by the end of the hold. Done right, the elbow should feel better for an hour or two afterwards — that immediate analgesic effect is the signature of a well-dosed isometric. If it feels like nothing at all, add weight.

02

Isometric Grip Hold

5 × 30 sec · dailyNew
Rolled towel or soft ball · 40–50% effort, elbow bent

Setup

Sit or stand with the elbow bent to 90° and tucked near your side, forearm neutral (thumb up). Hold a rolled hand towel or a soft stress ball. The bent elbow is deliberate — it shortens the extensor group and makes the position much more tolerable.

Movement

Squeeze at roughly 40–50% of your maximum effort and hold for 30 seconds, breathing normally. Release slowly. Rest 45 seconds between holds.

Form Check

The wrist stays in line with the forearm — don't let it flex or cock back as you squeeze. If you can't judge 40%, squeeze hard once to calibrate, then work at a level that feels comfortably sustainable for the full 30 seconds.

Tip This one directly rehearses the thing that hurts — gripping. Reclaiming it early, at a dose you control, is far better than avoiding grip entirely and meeting it again unprepared on the end of a shovel.

03

Forearm Extensor Stretch

3 × 30 sec · 2×/dayNew
Elbow fully straight — this matters

Setup

Extend the affected arm straight out in front of you at shoulder height, elbow completely locked out, palm facing down.

Movement

With your other hand, gently bend the wrist downward until you feel a pull along the top of the forearm. Add a slight tilt of the hand toward the little-finger side to deepen it. Hold 30 seconds, release, repeat three times.

Form Check

The elbow must stay straight — bending it slackens the muscle group and the stretch does nothing. You want a broad pull through the forearm, never a sharp pinch at the bony point of the elbow.

Tip Stretching alone doesn't fix a tendinopathy — the loading work does. Its job here is symptom relief and keeping the tissue mobile. Best used after your isometrics and after any session that aggravated things.

04

Wrist & Forearm Mobility Warm-Up

2 × 10 each · before sessionsNew
No load · pain-free range only

Setup

Sit with the forearm supported on a table or your thigh, hand free off the end. No weight in the hand.

Movement

Work through three motions, 10 slow reps each: wrist up and down through comfortable range; palm-up to palm-down rotation with the elbow pinned at your side; and slow open-and-close of the fingers into a loose fist.

Form Check

Move only as far as is comfortable — this is circulation and warm-up, not a stretch. Keep the elbow tucked during the rotations so the shoulder doesn't take over.

Tip Two minutes of this before your isometrics, and before any gym session, makes the first working set noticeably more comfortable. It's the cheapest thing on this page.

Grip-Sparing Setup for Shoulder Sessions

Every shoulder sessionNew
Straps on pulling · cuffs on rotation work

Setup

Bring lifting straps and a D-ring ankle cuff to every shoulder session. Both are cheap and both live in your gym bag from now on.

Movement

03

FlexBar Isometric Twist Hold

4 × 30 sec · dailyNew
Red bar (lightest) · hold the twist, don't unwind it

Setup

Hold the FlexBar vertically in the affected hand at the bottom, wrist cocked back into full extension. Grip the top of the bar with your other hand, palm facing away from you.

Movement

Use the good hand to twist the top of the bar, putting a moderate wind into it. Then simply hold that twist still for 30 seconds — your affected wrist's only job is to resist unwinding. Release gently. Four holds.

Form Check

The affected wrist stays extended and motionless. Shoulders down and relaxed — it's easy to hunch and shrug into this. Start with the red bar regardless of how strong you feel.

Tip This is the Tyler Twist position without the eccentric. Spending a week here means that when you start the real thing in Week 3, the only new variable is the lowering — not the grip, the position, and the load all at once.

04

Supination & Pronation Isometric

3 × 30 sec each way · dailyNew
Hammer held short · elbow pinned at side

Setup

Elbow bent 90° and pinned against your side. Hold a hammer up near the head — a short lever, so very little torque — or a light dumbbell gripped at one end. Forearm neutral, thumb pointing up.

Movement

Rotate the palm slightly upward and hold that position for 30 seconds. Then rotate slightly palm-down and hold 30 seconds. Three rounds of each direction.

Form Check

The elbow must not drift away from your ribs — if it does, your shoulder is doing the rotating. Slide your hand further down the hammer handle to make it harder, up toward the head to make it easier.

Tip Rotation is the forgotten half of forearm rehab, and it's exactly what a scraping or twisting task demands. Loading it now means you're not building a wrist that extends well but still can't twist.

Week 3 — Eccentric Phase
Slow lowering under load — the best-evidenced intervention for lateral epicondylalgia. Expect some ache during these; that's the treatment working, not damage. The isometrics drop back to a pain-relief tool.
01

FlexBar Eccentric Wrist Extension (Tyler Twist)

3 × 15 · dailyNew
Red bar · 4-second lower · the centrepiece of this program

Setup

Hold the bar vertically in the affected hand, wrist cocked back into full extension. Grasp the top of the bar with the other hand, palm facing away. Elbows bent, bar in front of your chest.

Movement

Twist the top of the bar with your good hand until it's fully wound. Keeping the twist, straighten both elbows out in front of you so the bar is horizontal. Now slowly let the affected wrist flex, unwinding the bar over a full 4 seconds. Reset and repeat, 15 times, three sets.

Form Check

The affected wrist only ever does the lowering. The good hand creates every bit of the twist. If your affected wrist is doing the winding you've reversed the exercise and lost most of its value.

Tip Bar progression is red → green → blue → black. Move up only when 3 × 15 feels genuinely easy and the next morning is quiet — usually two to three weeks per colour. This exercise is the single highest-yield thing on the page; if you only do one, do this.
Important Discomfort up to 4/10 during the set is expected and acceptable — eccentric tendon loading is meant to be mildly provocative. What is not acceptable is being clearly worse the next morning. If that happens, drop to 2 sets or go back a bar colour; don't push through it.

02

Eccentric Wrist Extension with Dumbbell

3 × 15 · dailyNew
1–3 lb to start · lower only, no lifting

Setup

Forearm flat on a table, palm down, hand and wrist hanging off the edge. Light dumbbell in hand. Use your other hand to lift the wrist up into full extension — that's the starting position.

Movement

Let the wrist lower slowly through a full 4-second count until it's fully flexed. Then use your other hand to lift it back to the top. You never lift the weight with the affected wrist — only lower it. 15 reps, three sets.

Form Check

Count the four seconds out loud the first few sessions; almost everyone lowers in two and thinks it was four. Forearm stays pinned to the table.

Tip This complements the FlexBar rather than duplicating it — the bar loads the tendon in a twisting pattern, the dumbbell in a straight one. Add weight in 1 lb increments only, and only when 3 × 15 is comfortable.

03

Eccentric Supination with Dumbbell

3 × 15 · dailyNew
Dumbbell held by one end · 4-second lower

Setup

Sit with the forearm resting on your thigh or a table, elbow at 90°, hand off the end. Grip a light dumbbell by one end only, so it's top-heavy and creates a rotational load. Start palm-up.

Movement

Week 5 — Heavy Slow Resistance
Eccentric-only work has done its job. Now load the tendon through the full lift-and-lower cycle with real weight and a slow tempo. This is the phase that actually rebuilds capacity — and it drops to every other day, because the load is genuinely heavy.
01

Heavy Slow Wrist Extension

3 × 12 · every other dayNew
3 sec up, 3 sec down · replaces the eccentric-only version

Setup

Forearm flat on a table, palm down, hand off the edge, dumbbell in hand. Start with the wrist relaxed into flexion.

Movement

Lift the weight into full wrist extension over 3 seconds, pause briefly at the top, then lower over 3 seconds. Six seconds per rep, 12 reps, three sets. Rest 2 minutes between sets. Every other day, not daily.

Form Check

Both halves of the rep are now yours — no assist from the other hand. Pick a weight where rep 12 of the last set is genuinely hard but your tempo doesn't collapse.

Tip Heavy slow resistance is now considered at least as effective as eccentric-only protocols, and most people find it far easier to stick with. Increase the weight when you can complete 3 × 12 with about two reps left in reserve.
Important Every other day is deliberate, not laziness. Tendons need roughly 48 hours to complete the collagen turnover this load triggers. Daily heavy work gives you the breakdown without the rebuild.

04

Radial Deviation with Hammer

3 × 12 · every other dayNew
Thumb-up · small range, slow tempo

Setup

Stand tall, arm hanging at your side, holding a hammer down the handle with the head pointing forward and the thumb up.

Movement

Tilt the wrist so the hammer head lifts upward — a small movement toward the thumb side — then lower it under control over 3 seconds. 12 reps, three sets.

Form Check

The elbow and shoulder stay completely still; only the wrist tilts. The range is small — a few inches of hammer-head travel is plenty.

Tip Extensor carpi radialis brevis, the tendon at the centre of tennis elbow, both extends and radially deviates the wrist. Most programs train only extension and leave this half untouched — which is one reason people plateau.

05

Loaded Carry (Farmer's Hold)

3 × 40 sec · every other dayNew
Single-arm · no straps · this one is meant to be gripped

Setup

A moderate dumbbell or kettlebell in the affected hand only. Stand tall, ribs down, shoulder blade set back and down — not shrugged up around your ear.

Movement

Simply hold it for 40 seconds, or walk slowly for 30 metres. Three sets, resting a minute between. Start conservative — this is a long-duration grip and it accumulates.

Form Check

Wrist neutral and straight, not letting the weight drag it into flexion. If your shoulder starts to hike or the shoulder blade loses position, the weight is too heavy — that matters given the dislocation history.

Tip This is the exercise that bridges the gap between clean rehab movements and real life — carrying tools, shopping, a bag. It's also excellent scapular work, so it earns its place in both programs at once.

Stand with a light dumbbell in each hand, arms at your sides, palms facing backward (pronated). Much lighter than your supinated curls — expect roughly half the weight.

Movement

Curl up over 2 seconds, lower over 3. Hold the wrist in slight extension the whole way — don't let it flop into flexion at the bottom. 12 reps, three sets.

Form Check

No swing, elbows pinned to your sides. The wrist position is the entire point of this exercise; if it collapses you're just doing a bad bicep curl.

Tip Pronated curls load the extensor origin hard, which is exactly why they belong at the end of the program rather than the start. They bridge to real-world tasks where you hold something heavy with the palm turned down — like a scraper.
Important Start absurdly light. This is the exercise most likely to cause a setback at this stage, because it feels easy on the biceps while loading the tendon heavily.

Task Exposure — Return to Tools

3 min, twice this weekNew
The actual task that flared it · short, deliberate bouts

Setup

Pick the real task — scraping, a screwdriver, wringing out a cloth, whatever your version is. Fit a thicker grip if the tool allows it: pipe insulation, self-amalgamating tape or a wrapped cloth all work, and on a twisting tool a fat grip genuinely reduces extensor demand.

Movement

Do exactly three minutes. Set a timer and stop when it goes, even if you feel fine — especially if you feel fine. Twice this week, with at least two days between. Then wait and see what the next mornings say.

Form Check

Keep the wrist neutral and let your shoulder and body weight drive the tool rather than your forearm. Swap hands or take a break rather than gritting through.

Tip This is graded exposure, and it's the step almost everyone skips. Six weeks of clean gym work does not automatically transfer to an afternoon of awkward, sustained, real-world tool use — that transfer has to be built deliberately, in doses.
Important Three minutes means three minutes. The failure mode here is feeling good at minute three and deciding to just finish the job — which is roughly the story of how this flared in the first place.

07

Maintenance Plan — Beyond Week 8

2×/week · ongoing 4–6 monthsNew
The part that decides whether this comes back

Setup

Tendon remodelling continues for months after symptoms settle. Eight weeks gets you comfortable; it does not get you finished. Plan on twice-weekly maintenance for at least another four to six months.

Movement

Keep three things: Heavy Slow Wrist Extension (3 × 10), the FlexBar Tyler Twist (3 × 15), and Heavy Slow Supination & Pronation (3 × 12). Twice a week, about ten minutes. Drop the rest, or fold the carries into your shoulder sessions.

Form Check

Keep progressing load slowly rather than holding steady. A tendon maintained at a fixed load slowly loses capacity — and eight months of low-grade symptoms is what that looks like.

Tip Before any known spike in demand — a weekend of DIY, a return to racquet sports — add a session or two in the fortnight beforehand. Preparing for a known load is far cheaper than recovering from it.
Important The commonest way this recurs is stopping when the pain goes. Symptoms improve weeks before capacity does. Stopping at the first quiet fortnight is precisely the mistake that produced the eight-month version of this.

Progress Milestones

On track

  • Morning stiffness trending down over a fortnight, even if individual days vary
  • Pain-free grip strength improving — you can squeeze harder before it complains
  • Ache during loading settles within an hour or two afterwards
  • Progressing a FlexBar colour or adding weight roughly every two to three weeks
  • Tool exposure doses increasing without a next-morning reaction

Back off or reassess

  • Clearly worse the next morning, repeatedly, at the same dose
  • No change at all in the trend after six to eight weeks of consistent loading
  • Pins and needles, numbness, or pain spreading down the forearm into the hand
  • Night pain, or pain that wakes you
  • Genuine weakness — dropping things, unable to hold a mug

What Would Make Me See Someone

Eight months of continuous symptoms is already past the point where an in-person assessment is worthwhile — not because this program is wrong, but because a physio or sports medicine doctor can confirm it's actually the tendon. Radial tunnel syndrome and a cervical (neck) contribution both mimic tennis elbow closely, are managed completely differently, and are exactly the things that make people plateau on a loading program that should have worked.

Someone watching you lift will also spot the grip and wrist habits that no written page can. If you've followed this consistently for six to eight weeks and the trend line is flat, that's the signal to go rather than to try harder.

On corticosteroid injections: they work well at six weeks and are associated with worse outcomes than physiotherapy or doing nothing at twelve months, with higher recurrence rates. If one is offered, that trade-off is worth discussing before accepting. Shockwave therapy and PRP are reasonable conversations for genuinely refractory cases; surgery is a distant last resort.
