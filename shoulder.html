Post-Dislocation · Axillary Nerve Recovery

Shoulder Rehabilitation Program

A progressive 3-phase program for greater tuberosity fracture recovery with axillary nerve involvement. 16+ months post-injury.

Cleared for full activity Nerve recovery ongoing No formal PT

Ground Rules

  • Pain during exercise: stay at or below 3/10. Sharp pain = stop immediately.
  • Pain after exercise: should not linger more than 60 minutes post-session.
  • Clicks/catches: OK to work through if they're not sharp or worsening.
  • Deltoid fatigues fast — take full 90–120 sec rest between sets, no exceptions.
  • Use higher reps (15–20) and lighter weight. Endurance before strength.
  • Progress only when you can complete all sets with pain ≤ 2/10.

Phase 1 — Restore Mechanics

Goal: re-establish humeral head centering, reduce stiffness and impingement, wake up the rotator cuff. 3 days per week with rest days between. Keep weights very light — this phase is about movement quality, not load.

Day A — Stabilizers + External Rotation (Mon / Wed / Fri)
🔄
Side-lying External Rotation
3 × 15Light dumbbell · 90 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Lie on your non-injured side. Bend your elbow to 90°, upper arm resting against your ribcage.
  2. Hold a very light dumbbell (1–3 lb to start). Keep your elbow pinned to your side.
  3. Slowly rotate your forearm upward toward the ceiling. Stop before any strain.
  4. Lower slowly — the lowering phase is equally important. Take 2–3 seconds down.
  5. Do NOT let your elbow lift away from your body.

Why it matters

  • Isolates infraspinatus and teres minor — the two muscles that pull the humeral head back into the socket during elevation.
  • This is the single most important exercise for reducing your impingement and catching sensations.
⚡ Nerve note: This exercise doesn't load the deltoid directly, so axillary nerve fatigue isn't a limiting factor here. You can push the full 3 sets confidently.

Prone Y-T-W Raises
2 × 12 eachBodyweight or 1–2 lb · 90 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Lie face-down on a firm surface or bench with arms hanging off the edge.
  2. Y: Raise arms diagonally overhead (like a Y shape) with thumbs up. Hold 2 sec at top.
  3. T: Raise arms straight out to the sides (like a T) with thumbs up. Hold 2 sec.
  4. W: Bend elbows to 90°, squeeze shoulder blades together and down, raising forearms. Hold 2 sec.
  5. Lower each slowly. These are small movements — don't rush them.

Why it matters

  • Trains lower trapezius and posterior rotator cuff together — essential for scapular control.
  • Corrects the upward humeral head migration that causes your painful arc.
💡 Start with zero weight. These are harder than they look. If 12 reps feels easy after 2 weeks, add 1 lb plates.

👊
Serratus Anterior Punches
3 × 15Bodyweight or light band · 60 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Lie on your back with arms extended toward the ceiling (shoulder-width apart, fists facing each other).
  2. Keeping your arms straight, push your fists further toward the ceiling by protracting (rounding forward) your shoulder blades.
  3. You should feel your shoulder blade lift off the floor/surface slightly.
  4. Slowly lower back down until shoulder blades touch the surface again.
  5. This is a small movement — about 2–3 inches of travel.

Why it matters

  • Serratus anterior keeps the scapula flat against the ribcage during arm elevation — without it, the scapula "wings" and impingement worsens.
  • Often the most neglected muscle in shoulder rehab programs.

📐
Scaption (Diagonal Raises)
3 × 121–3 lb dumbbell · 90 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart. Hold a very light dumbbell in your injured arm.
  2. Turn your arm so thumb points slightly up (not fully up, not fully down).
  3. Raise your arm at a 30° angle forward from straight out to the side — halfway between a front raise and a lateral raise.
  4. Raise only as high as comfortable — start with 90° (shoulder height) and don't force range.
  5. Lower slowly over 3 seconds.

Why it matters

  • This diagonal plane is where the supraspinatus is mechanically strongest — less impingement risk than pure lateral raises.
  • Trains deltoid and supraspinatus together in a joint-friendly arc.
⚡ Nerve note: Your deltoid may fatigue after 6–8 reps. If quality drops, stop the set early. Do not grind through deltoid fatigue — it compromises joint mechanics.

Phase 2 — Build Strength

Goal: add load progressively to stabilizers, begin compound pressing patterns, increase range of motion through full arcs. 3–4 days per week. Add Phase 1 exercises as warmup before each session.

Day A — Upper Pull + Rotator Cuff
🎯
Face Pulls
3 × 20Cable or band · 60 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Attach a rope to a cable machine or wrap a band around a post at face height.
  2. Grasp both ends, step back so there's tension. Start with arms extended.
  3. Pull toward your face, leading with your elbows flaring out wide and up.
  4. At peak contraction: elbows should be at or above shoulder height, hands near ears, external rotation at maximum.
  5. Hold 1 second at the end, return slowly over 3 seconds.

Why it matters

  • Trains posterior deltoid, rear deltoid, and external rotators simultaneously.
  • One of the best compound exercises for your specific situation — works around the axillary nerve limitation while still loading the shoulder meaningfully.
⚡ Nerve note: The rear deltoid (trained here) has partial innervation from branches separate from the main axillary nerve trunk — it may respond better than the lateral deltoid. This is a key exercise for you.

💪
Cable External Rotation at 90°
3 × 15Light cable · 90 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Set cable to elbow height. Stand sideways to the machine, injured arm facing away.
  2. Raise your elbow to shoulder height, bent at 90°. This is your start position.
  3. Keeping the elbow fixed at 90° and shoulder height, rotate your forearm backward (externally) as far as comfortable.
  4. Return slowly. The elbow does not move — only the forearm rotates.

Why it matters

  • Loads the infraspinatus and teres minor in the position where they work hardest — with the arm elevated.
  • Progresses the side-lying external rotation from Phase 1 into a more functional range.

Day B — Pressing + Deltoid
📍
Landmine Press
3 × 12Light plate load · 90 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Anchor one end of a barbell in a corner or landmine attachment. Grip the other end with one hand.
  2. Stand in a half-kneeling position (knee on the same side as your pressing arm is down).
  3. Start with the bar at shoulder height, elbow tucked, pressing at a 45° upward angle.
  4. Press until arm is almost fully extended. The bar travels in a slight arc — follow it naturally.
  5. Lower slowly and controlled. Don't let it drop.

Why it matters

  • The angled trajectory significantly reduces impingement risk versus overhead or flat pressing.
  • Single-arm pressing forces scapular stabilization — you can't cheat with the other side.
💡 Start with just the bar sleeve (no plates). The angle gives natural resistance. Add a 2.5 lb plate only when the bar alone is easy for all 3 sets.

↗️
Cable Front Raise
3 × 15Light cable, low pulley · 90 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Attach a single handle to a low cable pulley. Stand facing away from the machine.
  2. Grip the handle with your injured arm, palm facing down or inward.
  3. With a slight elbow bend, raise your arm forward to shoulder height (90°). Don't go higher initially.
  4. Hold briefly at the top, lower slowly over 3 seconds against the cable tension.
  5. The cable maintains constant tension throughout — unlike dumbbells which get harder at the top.

Why it matters

  • Constant cable tension is gentler on an unstable joint than dumbbell momentum.
  • Trains anterior deltoid and upper pec through a controlled, supported arc.
⚡ Nerve note: Monitor carefully — front raises are a primary deltoid movement. Stop the set if form degrades before completing all reps.

Phase 3 — Functional Strength

Goal: build real-world shoulder strength and overhead capacity. Progress weight gradually, maintain pain rules strictly. 4 days per week with one full rest day between each pair. Continue Phase 1 exercises as warmup.

Progressive Overhead Work (introduce carefully)
🏋️
Dumbbell Incline Press
3–4 × 12Moderate weight · 90 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Set bench to 30–45° incline. Lie back with a dumbbell in each hand at shoulder level.
  2. Press both dumbbells up and slightly inward — they should come together at the top, not straight up.
  3. Lower slowly, elbows at roughly 60° from your body (not flared 90° out).
  4. Stop the descent when upper arms are parallel to the floor — don't force a deeper stretch.

Why it matters

  • The incline angle is more shoulder-friendly than flat pressing for rotator cuff involvement.
  • Bilateral pressing lets you monitor side-to-side strength discrepancy — a key marker of nerve recovery.
💡 Track the weight difference between sides. As the axillary nerve recovers, the gap should gradually narrow over months. This is one of your best progress markers.
▶ Search video guide
🔝
Half-Kneeling Single Arm Press
3 × 10Dumbbell or cable · 90 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Kneel on the knee of your injured side. Hold a dumbbell in the injured arm at shoulder height.
  2. Brace your core. Press the dumbbell straight overhead — don't let your torso lean away.
  3. Lower slowly. The half-kneeling position prevents you from "helping" with your lower back.
  4. Only go as high as you can without pain. If full overhead isn't there yet, press to eye level and build from there.

Why it matters

  • Forces true overhead shoulder strength without compensation patterns.
  • The kneeling position eliminates leg drive — all load goes through the shoulder.
  • Best exercise to gauge readiness for full overhead activity.
⚡ Nerve note: You may not be ready for full overhead pressing for several more months. That's completely normal at this stage of axillary nerve recovery. Work to eye level, then forehead level, then overhead progressively — don't rush this.

🪢
Band Pull-Aparts
3 × 20Light resistance band · 60 sec rest
▾

How to perform

  1. Hold a resistance band in front of you with both hands, shoulder-width apart, arms extended at chest height.
  2. Keeping arms straight, pull the band apart by moving both hands outward until the band touches your chest.
  3. Squeeze your shoulder blades together at maximum stretch. Hold 1 second.
  4. Return slowly. Maintain tension in the band throughout — don't let it snap back.

Why it matters

  • High-rep band work builds the posterior shoulder endurance needed for daily function.
  • Works rear deltoid, rhomboids, and middle trap — all underactive in shoulder injuries.

Progress Tracking Milestones
Signs you're progressing well: Less catching/clicking during raises · Increased pain-free range of motion · Stronger lateral raise on injured side · Narrowing gap between sides on incline press · Deltoid no longer fatigues after just a few reps
Signs to scale back: Pain worsening after sessions · New clicking patterns · Increased aching at rest · Any pins and needles or increased numbness → contact your nerve doctor if neurological symptoms change
